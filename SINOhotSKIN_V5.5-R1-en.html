1. Background & Design Objectives

1.1 Marine Recovery Advantages Review

Compared to land-based recovery, marine recovery offers flexible landing zones, lower safety risks, minimal infrastructure investment, reduced structural loads, and greater mission adaptability — especially for large reusable spacecraft and high-inclination orbits. Key engineering challenges include:

  • Net impact / splashdown energy absorption
  • Floating stability and attitude control on water
  • Long-term corrosion protection in high-salt, seawater immersion
  • Post-recovery rapid inspection and modular replacement

This solution addresses these challenges with an integrated five-in-one armor system: Thermal Protection — Impact Absorption — Buoyancy — Sealing — Corrosion Resistance.

2.2 Buoyancy & Sealing Integration (Concept)

Buoyancy Sources:

  • Hollow truss sealed cavities
  • Closed-cell honeycomb rubber foam
  • Additional closed-cell foam buoyancy blocks on leeward side
  • Bottom module with integrated inflatable buoyancy bags (optional)

Preliminary estimates indicate the armor itself provides ~20 tons of buoyancy — insufficient for a 100+ ton vehicle. An external inflatable buoyancy system is required.

Supplemental Buoyancy System (Concept):

  • Inflatable buoyancy bags housed within armor cavities, automatically or remotely deployed upon water contact.
  • Total bag volume ≥100 m³ (scaled to re-entry mass), ensuring total buoyancy exceeds vehicle weight.
  • Multiple independent bags distributed circumferentially — single bag failure does not compromise overall buoyancy.
  • Bag material: multi-layer composite fabric (aramid/basalt fiber reinforced polyurethane) — seawater-resistant, UV-resistant, reusable.

Sealing Design:

  • All armor joints sealed with high-temp sealant + silicone rubber double seal.
  • Internal critical equipment bays independently sealed to IP68.
  • The stainless steel hull itself is a welded sealed structure providing basic watertight integrity.
  • All openings (strap grooves, anchor points) filled with flexible sealant.

3. Marine Recovery-Specific Design

3.1 Impact Attenuation System

Net Capture Mode:

  • Recovery net made of high-strength synthetic fibers, with pre-tension adjustable, max deflection ≥0.5 m.
  • Upon net contact, honeycomb rubber foam deforms to absorb energy, followed by net deflection to extend deceleration stroke.
  • Combined buffer system keeps peak acceleration well below 15 g — on the order of 2–3 g.

Emergency Splashdown Mode:

  • Relies on thickened honeycomb rubber in the bottom module + auxiliary inflatable bags.
  • Requires early deployment of drogue parachute or pre-inflated bags to reduce vertical velocity to ≤3 m/s, keeping combined acceleration within 15 g.

3.2 Recovery & Towing Interfaces

  • Four primary lifting points pre-installed on the stainless steel hull — each rated ≥50 t, for crane recovery.
  • Lifting point structure: welded stainless steel lugs, passing through armor via disposable TPS covers.
  • Towing points for mooring during recovery operations.
  • Local armor reinforcement around lifting points prevents stress damage.

3.3 Rapid Inspection & Modular Replacement

  • Single panel replacement: release local straps — design target ≤30 min.
  • Bottom module replacement: quick-release bolts — design target ≤2 h.
  • Buoyancy bag inspection: via external maintenance ports.

4. Materials & Structural Detail (Conceptual)

4.1 Panel Geometry & Fixing

  • Cylindrical windward side: square ceramic panels ~300 mm per side (order of magnitude), mortise-tenon joints, strap grooves on underside.
  • Nose cone: hexagonal ceramic panels ~300 mm per side, three-tenon/three-mortise, underside grooves.
  • Bottom module: square panels with thickened buffer layer — several hundred pieces.
  • Leeward side: flexible rectangular panels — no ceramic, basalt fabric + high-temp coating, thickness ~10+ mm.

4.2 Strap System

  • Material: alumina-based ceramic fiber braided strap, width ~10 mm.
  • Pre-tension: circumferential ~tens to hundreds of N, axial slightly lower.
  • Anchoring: composite anchor blocks welded or bolted to stainless hull, spacing ~600 mm (panel-optimized).
  • Thermal compensation: high-temp alloy spring elements with insulating washers.
  • Total length determined by panel count.

4.3 Dual Steam Cavities

  • Water volume: heat-flux matched — ~0.5–1.0 L/m², total several hundred liters.
  • Water bags: basalt fiber woven, high-temp resistant, dual independent cavities.
  • Refill interface: ground refillable, reusable.

5. Mass & Performance Assessment (Public Summary)

5.1 Mass Budget

Total armor mass ~26 tons — ~20% of re-entry mass. Thermal protection and buffer layers account for the largest share, followed by buoyancy system and structural adaptation.

5.2 System-Level Mass Offset

Marine recovery allows elimination or simplification of landing legs, retropulsive propellant, precision landing navigation equipment, etc. — saving several tons. Net mass increase is ~20 tons (~16%), which is acceptable.

7. Conclusion

This solution builds on V5.4-R2 with comprehensive enhancements for marine recovery:

  1. Enhanced Buoyancy: External inflatable bag system ensures ≥72-hour flotation.
  2. Complete Impact Attenuation: Combined honeycomb rubber, inflatable bags, and recovery net — peak acceleration kept safe.
  3. Robust Sealing & Corrosion Protection: Multi-layer sealing, IP68 critical bays, ≥10-year salt spray life.
  4. Optimized Recovery & Maintenance: Modular panels enable rapid at-sea operations.
  5. Controllable System Mass: Net mass increase ~20 tons — acceptable for full marine recovery capability.

This solution continues SINOSHOP's engineering philosophy: "Unlock the full potential of materials through the most intelligent architecture." It provides a conceptual technology pathway for marine recovery of Starship-class reusable spacecraft.
